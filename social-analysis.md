# Social Analysis: Upstream Drivers & Systemic Dynamics

## 1. Executive Summary
The practice of uncoordinated psychiatric discharge ("streeting") across Iowa shifts acute medical vulnerabilities onto under-resourced community entities. By releasing involuntarily committed patients without warm handoffs or secure medication supplies, the current system accelerates a compounding cycle of housing instability, criminalization, and clinical deterioration. Resolving this crisis requires replacing passive institutional drop-offs with mandatory, accountable community-care integration.

## 2. Social Determinants Framework
* **Economic Stability Factors:** Uncoordinated discharges exacerbate localized housing cost burdens and employment termination. Patients discharged directly to the street lack the structural stability required to maintain treatment regimens, driving an over-representation of severe mental illness (SMI) within unhoused populations.
* **Neighborhood & Built Environment:** Discharges frequently target municipal homeless shelters or street coordinates without prior facility notification or resource mapping, creating regional safety vacuums and resource deserts.
* **Social & Institutional Context:** Current execution models actively isolate individuals under involuntary commitment orders from their designated legal guardians, family care structures, and mobile crisis teams, substituting collaborative community protection with fragmented, siloed institutional handoffs.

## 3. Systems Theory Map
* **System Inputs (The Pressures):** Severe psychiatric bed shortages, immediate acute care hospital capacity pressures, and structural underfunding of long-term state Mental Health Institutes (MHIs).
* **System Mechanisms (The Engine):** Emergency department-driven intake loops that stabilize acute symptoms rapidly without treating long-term neurodevelopmental baselines, followed by a complete cessation of communication with outpatient stakeholders upon discharge.
* **System Outputs (The Cumulative Harm):** Accelerated "SMI churn"—a repeating cycle of emergency department visits, homelessness, county jail incarceration, and preventable deaths by suicide.

## 4. Systemic Abstraction Audit
* **Primary Structural Actor Type A:** Acute care private hospital networks operating under municipal tax exemptions that prioritize rapid bed-turnover over longitudinal aftercare stabilization.
* **Primary Structural Actor Type B:** Regional administrative service organizations (ASOs) managing public behavioral health allocations without standardized, publicly auditable tracking for post-hospitalization continuity.
